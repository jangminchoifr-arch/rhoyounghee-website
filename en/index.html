“It’s Okay! I’m a Hole-Bird”

Artwork by Rho Young-hee · Swipe to browse

Her first story collection and the book closest to her heart. Rho Young-hee both wrote the stories and drew every colored-pencil illustration on graph paper.

A Bird with a Hole in Its Chest

The title character is a bird cast in bronze—a cool, solid body with a wide-open hole right through the center of its chest.

Empty Enough to Embrace

Having an empty space was never a flaw. Because its chest is open, it can shelter others and hold them close—becoming the warmest bird of all.

Things That Think and Dream

A walking bronze statue, a little train bound for the sea, a nest of yellow-throated buntings… Eight philosophical tales give voice to quiet things so young readers can meet their own questions with gentleness.

“Father’s Wandering Song”

Seen through a child’s eyes, the quiet return of a father who once left without a word.

“The Future Lives in Our House”

A playful, tender story about welcoming tomorrow’s uncertainty with open curiosity.
“Someone named Future lives in our house. No one has ever seen their face clearly, yet every morning they leave a tiny spark of wonder on the kitchen table.”Adong Munye No. 466 (March 2025)

“Bicycle Shadow”

A lonely child in a new neighborhood finds courage by racing ahead with a stretching bicycle shadow.
“I ride my bicycle. As I pedal, the shadow that once clung timidly beside me begins to grow—arms stretching, legs lengthening, both wheels sweeping forward until I feel like a giant.”Adong Munhak Pyeongron No. 168 (Autumn 2018)

From Bedtime Storyteller to Children’s Book Author

Rho Young-hee
Born in Daegu, South Korea
Graduate Studies, Daegu University

Bedtime stories invented for her son first appeared in print in Samtoh magazine (1996) and led to her formal literary debut through the Maeil Shinmun New Writer’s Award in 2004. For more than twenty years, she has published regularly in Korea’s leading literary journals.

She writes contemplative, philosophical fairy tales that encourage children to ask how to live with kindness. Breathing life into inanimate things—a bronze bird, a little train, a growing island—she helps young readers face loneliness and longing with warmth. She also illustrates her own books.
